Place of Water

Bolgart Hall Mural: A 37-Metre Story

The wall begins at the western end, closest to the reserves and the old road that carried travellers from New Norcia to Toodyay. Here the landscape is ancient. Wandoo and marri rise tall, their white trunks catching morning light. In the canopy above, a flock of Carnaby's black cockatoos banks across the sky, their white tail panels flashing against dark feathers. They are the first thing you see from the road, large and unmistakable, a statement that this mural belongs to the Wheatbelt.

Below the canopy, the understorey is dense and alive. Woolly bush and parrot bush crowd between the trunks. A splendid fairy-wren perches on a low branch, his turquoise feathers almost electric against the muted greens. A red-capped robin sits lower still, a flash of crimson on a brown body, watching. At the base of the wall, where the paint meets the ground, grass trees stand like sentinels. Their blackened trunks and spiky crowns are the oldest thing on this wall, plants that grow a centimetre a year and have been here longer than the town.

In the shadows of the wandoo, a chuditch moves through the leaf litter. Western Australia's largest native carnivore, rufous brown with white spots, the size of a small cat. It is the animal most people have never seen but know is there. Painted half-hidden in the dappled light, its spots catching the faintest glow.

As the wall moves eastward, the woodland thins and the ground dips. This is where the water lives. Bolgart means place of water, and a spring rises here, painted as a ribbon of teal and blue that runs down out of the wall towards you. It runs the way this water runs: the springs feed the Toodyay Brook, and the brook heads south down the valley to the Avon at Toodyay, then on to the Swan and the sea. The water catches the light. Around it, the earth is soft and green. A Hills Salmon Gum stands at the centre of the composition, its smooth pale bark glowing against the darker trees around it. It is the hero of this section, tall and calm.

At the base of the salmon gum, white spider orchids push up through the leaf litter. Their delicate petals are painted with fine detail, the kind of thing you only notice when you walk up close. Beside them, eremophila drummondii blooms in blue-mauve, tubular flowers catching the light. This is the section where the community's nominated plants live, the ones people recognised and wanted included.

The golden wheat takes over as the wall moves into its third section. This is Bolgart's story as a farming town. Wheat stalks rise from the foreground, their heads heavy and golden, painted in warm ochre and cream. They are not decorative. They are the reason the town exists. Behind the wheat, the CBH silos and bulkheads rise above the paddocks: two dark corrugated steel bins with the elevator tower and its conveyor running over their tops, the long white shed alongside, and the angled steel walls of the bulkheads in front. It is the shape that defines Bolgart's skyline. The wheat stalks are woven through the lower third of the wall, dense and textured, giving way to open sky above. Beside the wheat, spring turns whole paddocks yellow with canola in bloom, a second gold so bright it fights the sky for attention. In the crop itself the harvest is under way: a tractor on its furrow with the header working behind it, trucks running the road with the load on, the day the town stops.

A windmill rises over the wheat, steel wings turning, pumping water from a bore into a tank the way this district has done for generations. It is another thread back to the spring, the thing that named the town, still working at the edge of the fields. Merinos graze the stubble and the green runs between the crops, the flocks that carried Bolgart alongside its wheat: wool in the sheds, lambs in spring, shearing days on the calendar. A sheepdog works the mobs, the sheep settling in the yards, and the Angus cattle stand down on the hill.

Into this working country, the town's own story rides in. Grain trucks stand loaded at the field's edge, ready to cart the harvest off to the silos, the last run the wheat makes before it reaches storage. Up the road, the general store holds its place in the story, where Fred and Jean Broomhall traded after 1937 and Jack Turner carried on through the 1940s, the counter where the town met its news.

A wedge-tailed eagle soars across this sky, wings spread wide, riding the thermals. It is the largest bird on the wall, painted with enough scale that you feel it overhead. Below the eagle, the wheat fields open up. Everlastings carpet the ground here, low and spreading, waves of pink, yellow and white close to the earth. They are the wildflowers that cover the Shire in early summer, the ones people drive from Perth to see. They fill the gaps between the wheat and the trees, softening the transition from farmland to bushland.

A bearded dragon sits on a rock in the foreground, its throat puffed, watching the sky. It is the only creature in this section, alone in the open wheat country where the trees have given way to paddocks. The dragon is a link between the bushland behind and the wildflower country ahead, and the whole wall reads that way: farming and the native bush in one story.

The final section of the wall moves into wildflower country. The wheat gives way to native scrub. York gum and white-barked wandoo tower at the back, their trunks tall and straight. In the foreground, cat paws cluster in groups, their fuzzy red and green flowers on slender stems. Cottonheads rise beside them, white and delicate. Trigger plants grow between rocks, their small flowers easy to miss unless you are close. Donkey orchids push up at ground level in the scrub, and after rain the gilgies hold shallow water in the rock.

A Christmas tree dominates the right side of this section, the Nuytsia in full summer bloom, its golden-orange flowers blazing against the sky. It is the most colourful thing on the wall, a tree that lights up the landscape when everything else has dried off. Beneath it, a short-beaked echidna noses through the leaf litter, its spines painted in fine detail. The echidna is the last animal on the wall, close to the ground, close to the viewer, the one children will look for first.

In the far background, the land fades into the wheatbelt horizon, soft and hazy. On the hilltops off Bolgart West Road and around Wattening, the stands of salmon gums, wandoo and casuarina take the last of the light. The wall ends here, at the eastern edge, where the reserves meet the farms and the wildflowers meet the wheat.

The whole mural reads as one continuous landscape. From the road it is bold and readable: dark cockatoos, a soaring eagle, a blazing Christmas tree, golden wheat, the silos and bulkheads on the skyline, grain trucks at the field's edge, a windmill turning over the paddocks. Up close it rewards attention: spider orchids in the leaf litter, a fairy-wren in the scrub, a chuditch half-hidden in shadow, a sheepdog working in the yards, a mob of merinos at the fence line, the general store counter, an echidna at the base. The water ties it together. It rises at the spring in the middle of the wall, the springs that named the town and fed the Toodyay Brook, and it runs down towards whoever is standing there, on its way to the Avon, the Swan and the sea. The windmill draws it up from the bore, and the gilgies hold it in the rock after rain. Bolgart, place of water.
